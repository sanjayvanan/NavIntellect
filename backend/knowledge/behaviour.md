# Reading ship behaviour

## Typical speeds by ship type
Rough cruising speeds: large tankers and bulk carriers 11 to 15 knots; container ships 16 to 22 knots (often slower to save fuel); general cargo 10 to 15 knots; car carriers 16 to 20 knots; ferries 15 to 25 knots and high-speed craft 25 to 40 knots; tugs up to about 12 knots; fishing boats 8 to 12 knots on passage and 2 to 5 knots while trawling. A speed far outside the range for the type (a tanker at 30 knots, a ferry drifting for hours) suggests wrong static data, a wrong ship type or unusual activity.

## Loitering
Loitering means staying slow (under about 1 knot) or moving in small circles for a long time. It is normal at anchorages, at fishing grounds, while waiting for a pilot or a berth, and for survey or support vessels. It is unusual in a shipping lane, in a restricted zone, near cables, pipelines or military areas, or close to another slow ship far from port.

## AIS gaps and dark activity
An AIS gap is a period in which a ship sends nothing. Most gaps are technical: the ship left terrestrial coverage, satellite reception was poor, or the equipment failed. "Going dark" means switching AIS off on purpose, which ships are allowed to do only when transmitting would endanger them (for example in piracy areas). Gaps worth a closer look: inside or near a watched zone, a ship reappearing far from where it went silent (check whether the distance is possible at its speed), repeated gaps in the same place, or gaps that match a meeting with another ship.

## Ship-to-ship transfers
In a ship-to-ship (STS) transfer, two ships lie alongside each other to move cargo, usually oil. Signs on AIS: two ships both slowing to under about 1 to 2 knots within half a nautical mile of each other for several hours, often away from ports, sometimes with one or both going dark. STS transfers are legal and common in designated areas, but they are also a known way to hide where cargo comes from and to avoid sanctions.

## Spoofing and false positions
AIS positions can be faked. Signs: a track that jumps impossible distances, positions on land, perfect circles or straight lines with no drift, many ships reported at the same spot, or the same MMSI in two places. Identity spoofing (using another ship's name, MMSI or IMO) shows up as identity data that does not match the ship's size, type or usual area.

## Anchorages and waiting areas
Ships wait at anchorages for a berth, for cargo, for bunkers or for orders, sometimes for days. Slow or stopped ships at a known anchorage with status "at anchor" are normal. A ship stopped at sea far from any anchorage, drifting with status "under way", is less usual.
