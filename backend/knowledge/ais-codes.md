# AIS codes

## Navigational status codes
The navigational status is set by the crew. 0 = under way using engine. 1 = at anchor. 2 = not under command. 3 = restricted manoeuvrability. 4 = constrained by her draught. 5 = moored. 6 = aground. 7 = engaged in fishing. 8 = under way sailing. 9 and 10 are reserved (for high-speed craft and wing-in-ground craft). 11 = power-driven vessel towing astern and 12 = power-driven vessel pushing ahead or towing alongside (regional use). 13 is reserved. 14 = AIS-SART, man overboard or EPIRB device active. 15 = undefined (the default when nothing was set). Crews often forget to change it, so a ship moving at 12 knots with status "at anchor" is common and usually a mistake rather than a sign of anything.

## Ship type codes
The ship type is a two-digit number. 30 = fishing. 31 and 32 = towing. 33 = dredging or underwater operations. 34 = diving operations. 35 = military operations. 36 = sailing. 37 = pleasure craft. 40 to 49 = high-speed craft. 50 = pilot vessel. 51 = search and rescue. 52 = tug. 53 = port tender. 54 = anti-pollution equipment. 55 = law enforcement. 58 = medical transport. 60 to 69 = passenger ships. 70 to 79 = cargo ships. 80 to 89 = tankers. 90 to 99 = other types. The second digit of the 60 to 90 groups marks hazardous cargo categories (1 to 4 = hazardous category A to D).

## IMO number
The IMO number is a seven-digit number given to a ship hull for its whole life, unlike the name, flag and MMSI, which can change when a ship is sold or reflagged. It is the most reliable identifier when present. The last digit is a check digit. Many small ships have no IMO number.

## Callsign
The radio callsign is assigned by the flag state. Its first characters show the country (for example 9V for Singapore, M or G for the United Kingdom). Like the name, it is entered by the crew.
